What is a Bitter solenoid?

A Bitter solenoid is an electromagnet built from a stack of thin copper plates instead of wound wire. Each plate is a flat ring (an annulus) with one radial cut, the slit. Thin insulating sheets sit between the plates. Each insulator leaves a small sector uncovered so that neighbouring plates touch there. Current flows almost once around a plate, crosses to the next plate through that contact sector, and goes around again. Plate by plate, the current follows a helical path up the stack, just like the turns of a coil. The design is named after its inventor, the physicist Francis Bitter.

One plate

The copper ring has a bore in the middle and a single radial slit (right). The blue circles are cooling holes. They line up from plate to plate and form straight water channels through the whole stack. The arrow shows the current going around the plate, from one side of the slit to the other.

Why the current density goes as 1/r

Inside a plate the current flows around the ring (the azimuthal direction) and fills its whole width. Going once around the plate drops the same voltage at every radius. The path at radius r is 2πr long, so the electric field along it is that voltage divided by 2πr. Current density is conductivity times electric field, so the azimuthal current density scales as 1/r. More current flows near the bore than at the outer edge. This puts the current close to where it makes the most field, but it also means the inner edge heats the most.

Cooling

A resistive magnet turns all of its electrical power into heat. In a Bitter magnet, water is pumped along the channels formed by the aligned holes. It touches the copper directly along the whole length of the stack, and that is what carries the heat away.

Why Bitter magnets are used for high fields

A strong field pushes outward on the current that makes it (the Lorentz force), which puts the conductor under large hoop stress. A clamped stack of solid plates is mechanically rigid and carries that stress through the plate material. Direct water cooling through the plates removes the heat. Together, these let Bitter-type magnets reach the highest steady fields of any resistive magnet. Examples are 41.4 T at the US National High Magnetic Field Laboratory in 2017, and 42.02 T at a power of 32.3 MW at the Chinese Academy of Sciences High Magnetic Field Laboratory in Hefei in 2024 (CAS news).

What this simulator does

It models a modest 0.5 T copper Bitter coil. From the plate geometry and the water flow, it computes the current needed for the field, the voltage and power, the water temperatures and pressure drop, the hottest copper temperature, and how uniform the field is at the centre. An optimiser searches for a low-power design that meets the limits. The demo runs the same model in your browser, and its numbers are checked against the Python code.
